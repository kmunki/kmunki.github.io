Waiting at the dock in Busan was the Olokun’s ship, the *Chombo*. Hours prior, Illapa had delivered the moto, but stayed away herself until she was sure she had no tail. Two heavily armed macaques eyed her as she strode up the gangplank. She held out her talisman, waved it in the pass-gesture, then spoke the mission password. The guards relaxed. Just beyond was Biby, wringing her hands. The ship began to pull out of the harbor as Illapa, their sole reason for docking, followed the lemur below deck.

When the *Chombo* was first broached as transport from Koryo, Illapa had asked why a great creature of the sea, a borderline aquatic demigod, needed an ocean craft. The Olokun had responded with a curt, “Do you walk everywhere?” But the real answer, Illapa realized, was the crew: crows, lemurs, cats, bats, and more than a few humans, run with military precision. Though there was still too much squawking and chirruping for her taste, a ship-as-headquarters was a savvy move.

Biby led her through a vast work area in the center hull, humans and hybrids bent to their intelligence work between partial bulkheads, the horizon moving subtly beyond the windows. Behind sliding paneled walls waited Vidya, a grey parrot, and one of the Olokun’s scientists, as well as the findings from the palace.

“Welcome back, Illapa,” said Vidya. “This,” she indicated the parrot, “is Iskandra, the Olokun’s proxy.”

“Pleasure,” said Illapa with a forced smile.

“For you, yes. For me, we shall see,” said Iskandra. “On this ship, I command. I’ve heard about your reputation—”

“Vidya! How hurtful,” said Illapa, feigning insult.

“There it is. As promised,” said Iskandra, irritated. She clacked across the table with a head-bobbing walk. “You Inka seem *still* to think hybrids are helpers, not peers. I am stopping that mistake. This”—the bird tilted her head to the side, and Illapa could see, just within the flared feathers, a glitter of wire—“is war-relic tech. *We* adapted it. I’m no uplift, but I’m close. Know that. Much as you dislike having a hybrid in charge, that is your situation.”

“On the boat you, off the boat me,” said Illapa.

“And where are we?” said the parrot, as if speaking to a toddler.

“My goodness, I hope on the boat.”

The parrot stood, staring, patient.

“Aye, mu’m-ji,” said Vidya, all but vibrating with discomfort. “We understand.”

“Captain, shall I?” said the scientist.

“Yes, Onosaʻi. What has our insouciant Inkan brought us?”

Onosaʻi had the thin, exhausted build stereotypical of her ilk. Her long, straight black hair was pulled back, more out of the way than in any particular style. To Illapa, she appeared Polynesian—though which islands was unclear. Intricate purple tattoos, whirls, squares, dots, and bands, covered her skin.

“Agent Illapa,” said Onosaʻi, “you had our crew load aboard a moto, a case, a cypher notebook, a blood sample, a segment of armor, and a patch. Why?”

Illapa debriefed them: the seditious guard, the hidden compartment in the old woman’s hut, the battle-damaged garage, her glorious departure.

“The moto itself was”—Illapa smiled at the thought—“*is* a masterpiece. Serpent feathers, it is fast. Even I was wary to open her up all the way. Whoever had piloted this in its heyday must have been…” Illapa whistled, at a loss.

“Must have been a war-relic themselves, as these are.”

“All of it?” Vidya asked, incredulous.

“The moto and case,” said Onosaʻi. “Near the end of the war. The moto frame is woven chitin. *Grown* that way. Exceptionally light and strong.”

“Property of Condor, of course,” said Illapa.

“Easy, Illapa,” said Vidya, “the Keralan-Inkan agreement, amended to include the Olokun, gives us *all* access.”

“We get first commercial rights.”

“And *we* get shadow market,” said Iskandra.

“This moto is not any war-relic,” said Onosaʻi, curt. “It’s likely one-of-a-kind; so secret that its records were destroyed upon creation. What you casually rode five hundred kilometers like it was some rental, Agent, might be the discovery of the decade.”

Illapa bowed with exaggerated pomp. “You’re welcome.”

“Unbelievable,” squawked the parrot. “The rest?”

“The case. We opened it with a secondary key within the fob. It contains a vajra rail.”

“A vajra!” Vidya lunged forward to see.

Onosaʻi opened the case. Illapa placed a hand on the display table, steadying herself, her knees suddenly weak. A vajra rail.

“You’re certain?” said Vidya, a skeptical hand hovering, afraid to touch it. “Even the Keralan archives only have a handful, and *we* invented it.”

“You have fewer than that,” said Iskandra.

Vidya’s eyes bugged.

“Well, this is a first,” said Illapa, “I agree with the bird.”

“A fair concern,” said Onosaʻi, forbearing. “The Keralan prototypes have one or two of the capabilities rumored of the vajra pistols. This one has all of them. The entire weapon, not simply the barrel or slide, is osso-ceramic.”

She lifted the bone-white weapon from its case with reverence, then presented it to Illapa on open palms. Illapa took it and gasped. Not cool, like metal, but room temperature, almost warm, and light, half the weight of her pistol. She held it aimed at the floor, pulled the slide back, then returned it to the case, the thrill of holding it running through her like voltage.

“An entire weapon of this quality is, speaking freely, unimaginable,” said Onosaʻi. “And the reliefs are of spectacular beauty.”

“Are these…” Vidya’s sentence trailed, her hand running over the intricate images carved into the weapon. Along it were motifs of nature: vines, flowers, branches, leaves, thorns, and seeds. Among and within them were other weapons: longbows, war-hammers, swords. Along the top a single magnificent club was centered: the vajra, holy bones made godly weapon. It was double-ended with clawed club heads. From each stretched gemmed lightning. The bolts were inlaid with diamonds, not only clear and white, but yellow, red, blue, each placed with an artisan’s eye so that, with the subtlest tilting, they flashed, as though a live charge coursed through the wielder’s hand.

“Celestial weapons,” said Illapa, finishing Vidya’s sentence, “divine arms that fought and won the wars of legend.” She looked up. “Sigils. Totems. As the war progressed, belief in—not magic, not precisely, but the harmonic and immaterial elements of the universe—was interwoven with science and reason.”

“Exactly right,” said Onosaʻi. She turned to the parrot. “Your agent is well read. Arrogant and reckless by reputation, the occasional churl, but with some erudition. These relics are the tallest feather in the Olokun’s crown.”

“Wait,” said Vidya. “How did the old Koryo rearer come into possession of these?”

“Gifts,” shrugged Illapa. “Theft? War-relics are found all the time. If I could manage it, chances are she could.”

“Or she is an asura,” said Iskandra.

Illapa and Vidya looked at the bird in shock. She gestured back to the scientist.

“The evidence,” said Onosaʻi, “is purely speculative. However”—she tapped the shard—“this adds to that theory. The segment is a carbon-chitin alloy, but clearly natural, grown. We believe it’s ogumo.”

“Ogumo!” Vidya sat, overwhelmed. Illapa’s face became stern.

“Any military would be wary to lose such an asset,” said Illapa, “even in peacetime.”

“Deep freeze being the most obvious method,” said Onosaʻi. “But this shows no signs of de- and rehydration strain on the cellular walls. Isotopic decay would indicate recent growth, in the last decade.”

“You’re saying someone is making *new* ogumo armor,” said Illapa.

“Not only making—*using* it,” said Vidya. “At the Koryo Summer Palace. An ogumo, even one, would have been more than a match for every guard there, as well as a former assassin and her reared protégés—were they simply *human*.”

“There is no count of asuras,” said Iskandra. “Bits and pieces. Guesswork. Violet’s Chief of Security is the only known post-war asura, and still”—she fluttered, frustrated—“just rumor.”

“And given the leaks from Koryo’s Viceroy-elect, Yi Ji-woo,” said Vidya, “there is substantially more interest in finding the Sifu and the Ward, Yi Hyeon Bong-cha, than Princess Seong-ma. The Ward and Sifu may have escaped.”

“Do we have any leads there?” Illapa asked.

“Only this.” Iskandra picked up the patch with a claw.

“The mercenaries it is, then? The Malians?”

“Yes,” said the parrot, bobbing her head with delight. “I get to send you into harm’s way once again, Agent Illapa. You’ll be meeting with them in Nairobi.”

Illapa grinned. “And I get off this ship.”

Vidya was now pacing, touching the objects, almost manic. She tapped the moto seat, affirming its corporeality, then gestured to the gun.

“Here is an object that should not exist. Not merely its material, but its quality, the beauty of the design and the detail of the inlay. But more than that, this is the White Weapon. It was wielded not by just any asura, but by the first, the world-killer, Ananda. Some called it the Moon, because in her hands it changed the tides of battles. For others, that whiteness was skeletal; exposed bone, bleached in the sun. A vision of the wastelands it could create in her hands. Ananda, what a name. Holding this gun is like being handed a lightning bolt or watching someone reach up and pluck a star out of the night sky. And where do we find it? In the hidey-hole of an old woman who tutors two girls whose names were on the list Brand found. My bedrock of reality is slipping.”

She turned to the parrot and the scientist. “Iskandra, Onosaʻi, I am no fool. The Inka and UKK have been sharing intelligence on you since the moment you came to our attention. We have conflicting goals. But Violet is our shared worry, and where our goals align for the time being, they align, no more or less.”

The parrot, agent, and scientist were quiet a moment.

“History is shifting around us,” said Vidya. “If we’re looking over our shoulder the entire time, it is likely we will miss the Old Gods emerging from the Shadow right in front of us. Or, perhaps by accident, we’ll unleash them, restarting the Long War a century after we thought it had ended.”

“I’m very careful,” said Illapa, sliding the patch into her pocket. “Let me know when the plan is done.”

“Where are you going?”

“I’ve been on the road for hours. I’m for a bath and bed before I’m shot at again. Speaking of,” she said, slapping Vidya on the shoulder, “don’t shoot me in the back while we’re making history. And make sure the bird doesn’t either, eh?”

She walked out and waved down a crew member for directions to the staterooms. Then she poked her head back into the room. “And you,” she said, pointing to Onosaʻi, “if you’d like to join for the bed, bath, or both, please do.”

She winked and was gone.
