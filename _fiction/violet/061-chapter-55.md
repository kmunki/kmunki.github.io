The agar film had tightened as it dried. Hye felt it at every expression she did not make, a second skin over the three slashes. The strapping around her ribs let her breathe in halves. She had not slept. From the look of the chamber, no one had.

Dawn came through the high lattice in gray bars. The four light-boards were dark, their inlaid stones dull as river pebbles taken out of the water. Beneath them the council sat in yesterday’s clothes, silk gone stiff where the rain had dried in it, hems still dark from the courtyard. The Chamber of Harmonic Discord had never been so quiet, and the quiet made it larger, a bowl with nothing yet poured in.

Ji-woo sat on the lowest tier between two of Ryong’s guards. The chrys-cast cupped his jaw from ear to chin, and the eye above it was swollen shut. The other eye moved. It went to the doors each time they opened and never once to Hye. He had not said a word since they carried him in, though the cast left room enough for a whisper. Someone, Hye guessed, had told him what would happen if he used it, and told him in a way he believed.

Daewoo presided from the Vizier’s chair. Beside her the Viceroy’s chair stood empty. She had not changed either; the jewels and charms sewn into her robes were dulled with water spots, and she wore them as though she had chosen them for the occasion.

“Councilors,” she said. “You will forgive the hour, and the state of us. I assure you no one in this room minds my robes more than I do.” A few tired breaths of laughter. “We will hear the witnesses. Then we will vote. Then, with any luck, we will sleep.”

Eshma came down to the witness floor first. She had no hookah, and her hands had not been told; they kept rising for a mouthpiece that was not there and settling instead on the lapels of the coat she wore, a councilor’s charcoal wool, too long in the sleeve, which on her looked commissioned. Her voice was a ruin. She had screamed it away in the Residence.

“Councilors.” A rasp, then she found what was left. “I have been fed in the palaces of maharajas. For four days I have been fed on herring in a refrigerator, listening to what had been your Saongwon cook your staff. You will forgive me if I am not at my most melodious. The first night, I was awake. One is always awake at the corpse of a party. Winji was with me. Winji is not here to say so. We heard the bolts go, all through the building, and I said, how charming, the Koryo are tucking us in. Some time later a woman came along the balcony from the Saongwon’s end, dressed for a funeral no one had yet had. Her eyes—” Eshma stopped. “There was nothing in them. No white at all. At the door she held up a little block of carved wood, two dragons fighting, and the guard bowed to her as though she were the Viceroy herself. And he let her out. Out, Councilors. Of a building he had just locked.”

Msizi testified the way she might have read a manifest. One lens of her octagonal spectacles was cracked corner to corner. The doors were barred from outside at twelve minutes past two; she knew because she had checked her watch; she did not say whose room she had been in. The thing came before dawn. Four days in the refrigerator. She laid their log on the rail, written on the backs of inventory sheets still curled from the cold, and read from it: the hours it hunted, the hours it cooked, the hours it slept, the times it ate.

“It ate,” she said, “at intervals. I can give you the intervals.”

Commander Ryong stood last.

“The Residence cameras went to closed loop the night the doors were barred. One camera can’t be looped. The armory’s. It’s built not to be tampered with, which in this palace is saying something. On the fourth day it shows three women arming themselves. The Ward, the Sifu, and the Inkan ambassador. Arming. Not feeding. Behind a false wall on the ground floor, by the far stair, there’s a safe room with its own recorder. It kept everything the loop was hiding, and it sends. We pulled it over the wire an hour ago. Four days. I have seen enough of all four. I don’t recommend it.”

High on the north wall, a single light came on, red. Its cardinal cousins answered on the other three.

“And the Saongwon’s terminal shows the isolation order given under the Viceroy-elect’s authorization.”

Another light. Then a scatter, then whole rows, the dark stones waking red around the room.

It was the Sifu’s plan, working. *Safe door. Outer doors. Alarm.* Hye had pressed the buttons herself; her thumbs remembered the safety catches. A Ward and a foreigner, sole survivors of a massacre they had every reason to cause, would have been called liars. Two ambassadors could not be. The room believed her this morning because a Rajput in a borrowed coat and an Onzulu with a clipboard had walked out of that building alive, and they had walked out because the Sifu stayed in the water.

“Ward Hyeon,” said Daewoo. “Do you wish to add anything?”

“No, Vizier.”

“Representative Msizi?”

“One thing.” Msizi took off her spectacles. “The woman with no whites to her eyes. The Onzulu know her. She passed our lower gates under a trade contract, on a date I will give the council in writing. No one in the arc could say who had signed it, or why she went where she went once inside. Last night I sent her description home. The arc answered at four this morning.” She let a breath go by. “The contract is with Violet.”

It was the first time the word had been spoken in that chamber since Hye had thrown it at Ji-woo a week before. On the lowest tier, his one eye closed.

“The Onzulu, it appears, signed it,” said Msizi. “The Onzulu would very much like to know who held the pen.”

The question came, as it had to, from an old councilor on the fourth tier. He did not stand. “And what, precisely, does the Vizier propose Koryo do about a Sovereignty?”

Daewoo drew her hands into her sleeves.

“Name it, Councilor. We have now done so, before the Rajput Alliance and the Onzulu. Refuse it: its contracts, its agents, its goods in our ports. That we can do by noon.” Her hat jangled as she shook her head. “Try it? We cannot. There is no court it will stand before. A Sovereignty is a nation that has decided not to be answerable. We will have to make it answer some other way.”

The doors opened hard. General Jee came down the aisle in fatigues gray with edge-land dust, her scars pulled white by the cold.

“I’m late,” she said to the room. “I was in the edge-lands winning ground for a Viceroy who was already dead. The army is at the station.”

Nobody thanked her. She took her seat on the first tier, three places from Ji-woo, and did not look at him.

The Master of Law rose. She did not bother with the scroll. “The Duel has been judged. The council will confirm.”

Every light on the four boards went red, north and south and east and west, and stayed red. Daewoo looked up at them a long moment. She did not move to the other chair.

“The ceremony is next week,” she said. “The chair will keep.”

Then the Master of Law did unroll the scroll, and turned to the lowest tier. “Yi Ji-woo. Your title of Viceroy-elect is struck.” She found the passage. “For treason against the throne by one of the Progeny, the penalty is death, and the name struck from the rolls.”

“Commuted,” said Daewoo, before the lights could move. “Confinement, pending the judgment of the Continuum.”

The boards stuttered white, abstaining, then went red again, row by row. A trial would need a charge, and a charge would need a co-conspirator, and someone would have to stand in this room, with the Rajput Alliance and the Onzulu listening, and say aloud who had owned the Viceroy-elect of Koryo. Better to lock the door and let the Continuum carry it.

Ji-woo’s shoulders dropped a finger’s width. It took Hye a moment to recognize it as relief.

---

Msizi found her in the corridor afterward.

“Illapa pressed this on me at the doors,” Msizi said. “Before she ran back inside. She said you, and no one else.” She held out a square of paper folded small and hard, as if it had spent the night in a fist.

Hye unfolded it. The hand was quick and slanted.

*Long version is in Nairobi. Ask the whale. She has a word for what they’re shipping.*

Hye read it twice. “What does it mean?”

“I don’t know. I asked. She was already running. I should tell you I have memorized it. The Onzulu will want to know what it means. I would rather you heard that from me.”

Hye folded the paper along its creases and put it inside her jacket, against the strapping. “Thank you.”

Msizi nodded once. Her hand went to her own breast pocket, found it empty, and stayed there a moment before she let it fall.

---

The Residence smelled of kerosene and wet ash and, under both, of the stockpots no one had yet been willing to empty. Tarps lay in rows along the grass outside, weighted at the corners with stones, and Hye did not count them. The soldiers at the door let her through because they did not know how not to.

The false wall by the far stair had sealed itself again when the alarm was cut. No one had told the soldiers there was anything to find. She pressed the panel and the safe room door hissed open on the dark and the blinking console, and something shrieked at her from three throats.

Trine sat among the torn foil of every emergency ration in the cabinet. They had eaten all of it, apparently out of spite, and were furious: three heads up, three sets of whiskers going, one tail knotted beneath them like a fist. Hye held out her hand. They considered it, then climbed on and ran up her sleeve to the back of her neck, where they bit her once, not hard, and settled.

“I know,” said Hye. “I know.”

In the great hall the water had drained, leaving a tidemark on the hardwood and a crust of ash on the carpet’s ornamental seal. The chandelier lay where it had fallen, crystals scattered to the walls like hail. Under it, the busaw: the plated back, charred black and split; the long arm, flung out; the skull, opened, with the cable still in it. It was only meat now, and it smelled like meat.

The Sifu was not there.

Where she should have been, the floorboards were holed in a tight cluster, brass casings in the ash around them. Someone had fired into that spot at close range, again and again, at something already dead.

“Ward.” A young guard had followed her in, helmet under his arm. “The Inkan took her. In a box. A coffin. It flew.” He made a shape in the air with his hand, up and over and away toward the sea, and seemed embarrassed by it. “We didn’t know if we should stop it.”

By noon the council would decide what that meant. Hye’s doing, some would say, the Ward spiriting the old asura’s body away from a reckoning. Others would call it the Sifu’s last trick. Hye found she did not mind which. Let them have it.

Trine shifted against her neck. Hye walked out past the tarps and the soldiers, into the thin winter light, and turned toward the edge of the palace, where the shrines were.
