*Our best lead is the Malian mercenary group Blade of Kahina. We’ve set up a meeting with you to speak with their corporate security representative, Sadiya ibnat Tanaz al-Mzab, under the guise of augmenting Condor Moray’s security. She is smart, knows it, and is proud of it. Arrogant. Preening. Extremely dangerous. The meeting has been arranged in city-lower. No cover identity. Try to avoid trouble. And gambling. Learn whatever you can about Violet. Good luck.*

Vidya’s misplaced prophylaxis against a bit of fun notwithstanding, the intel was good. And city-lower, how exciting! Her dalliances with Tir and the lemur had not left time to enjoy Nairobi’s full stack of delights. At the head of the escalators, a small woman presented a ticket to a gorilla, who turned, paused, and made a gesture of *after you*. Illapa rode the escalators down, nearly sixty stories of them, zigzagging like probing roots.

Humans are remarkably good at *not* being animals. What animals cannot do, however, is be repressed. An irritated human is, in some ways, several layers of abstraction wrapped around an irritated primate. An irritated hybrid chimpanzee is a single layer of abstraction wrapped around an irritated primate. That lone layer is language. And in city-lower, the humans were not much different. People were passionate, loud, vulgar, and very much *themselves*, regardless of their species.

Which is to say, it was not the *act* of the crows ordering a piece of pizza—it was the *argument* between two *species*, both equally animated and belligerent, that told Illapa she had truly arrived in city-lower.

The pizza-maker shouted that the crows had used up their credit line, to which the crows responded that she was, in fact, a jerk. They didn’t even like eating there. But they did eat it! That was a favor to the chef! Where was the credit for a favor? The crows talked over each other, piling phrases and ideas on top of one another. “Fine!” the pizza-maker yelled. “You want? Here!” She hurled a slice into the chasm between buildings. The crows launched after it. A brave one pulled her wings close and dove. She snatched the slice, which was nearly as large as she was, and, unable to hoist it, decelerated sufficiently and glided to a stairwell landing. Her compatriots joined her and began squawking and cawing, first victoriously, then angrily, at the question of how to divvy up the slice.

Matatus and tuktuks, painted riotous colors and barnacled with trinkets, made liberal use of chirping horns that augmented their drivers’ insults and exculpations. The final ingredient was the encroachment of the jungle from city-under. Paths were coated with almost invincible moss, signs dangled with vines, and ferns and huge-leafed greenery of all kinds found a way to thrive under the blaze of neon. The pervading humidity condensed onto the cool leaves and metal, creating a perpetual dripping.

Along the walkway, neon grenades flashed offers of bulk discounts, travel and meals included. Illapa tried to imagine what position she would be in where she could afford mercenaries but also just decide to drop in without an appointment, based on the enticement of “first two hundred rounds on us.” In Nairobi, you could window-shop your way to world power, if the signs were to be believed.

At the end of the block she found the sign, an old wooden plaque lit by two spotlights, more like a shingle for a pub than a prestige mercenary house. Then again, what *should* it have looked like? She went in.

Inside was a lone woman writing in a large ledger. The room was clean, dimly lit with soft orange light. The reception area had a Moroccan aesthetic of low couches, sitting cushions, and round tables.

The woman looked up. She was West African and wore a formal outfit like a paramilitary dress uniform. Not a hair of her Fulani high braided bun was out of place.

“One moment,” she said, then, gesturing to the couch, “Please.”

She returned to her writing. Her movements and posture were deliberate. The pen’s sweep across the paper was balletic and not without flourish. She finished, closed the ledger with a loud thump, moved it to a precise spot on the desk, and placed her pen in its holder, flaring her fingers as if sprinkling it there. She stood and came around from behind the desk.

“You must be Illapa of Condor Moray,” she said, extending a hand with a raised eyebrow and sly grin. “How I have been looking forward to meeting you.”

Illapa stood and they shook.

“I am Sadiya. Let’s move ourselves to the more interesting portion of the conversation presently: How can the Blade help a Sovereignty as big and powerful as Condor Moray? You have that Inkan military lurking just outside the picture. Unless…” Sadiya bobbled her head back and forth, as if she were contemplating something better left unsaid.

“Some things you leave to your top talent,” said Illapa. “Others, you contract out.”

Sadiya chuckled. “How mean you all have become since getting into bed with those nasty Keralans. You’ll come to regret that, I suspect. They rather disapprove of your methods. Can you imagine, no winnowing?”

“It is hard,” said Illapa, “seeing how many of my own brood didn’t survive to adolescence. Though things among the Sok-Oyo seem a bit tough even to me. Aren’t you worried about someone coming in here and strong-arming you out of a job?”

“They are welcome to try. A few have. Something I learned early is that though might makes right, ‘might’ is no one thing. Why, take yourself! You have come to me. You are a top spy, a vaunted field commander, and have the resources of the former Imperium at a word. Not a few days ago you stole a few goods, some *quite* valuable it seems, from my team in Koryo. All this might is not enough. Or, how shall I put this, not precisely what all my customers need.”

She smoothed her sleeve, though there was no crease in it.

“Take one of my specialties: trigger contracts. Let’s say you wanted to shoot me. I have three trigger contracts. Two are quite public—both involve an immediate and substantial dispensation of liquid funds to kill not only the person responsible, but to do ‘irrevocable harm’ to whoever stands to benefit by proxy. The third contract is secret, a dead-hand switch that only I and my serialized set of executors know about. Maybe it’s to blow this place up! Maybe it’s to release a swarm inside the city limits. No one knows but me. My might, here, comes from money, of course, but also a compilation of secrecy, chaos, and the imagination of my enemies.”

“You see, then, why I have come to you.”

Sadiya laughed, high and haughty. “Oh, delicious. You’ve gotten me to audition. A rare treat! There are only a few options: you’re lying”—she made an exaggerated pout—“the job isn’t worth your time, or—and I won’t lie, this last one is why I even spoke with you—you need me to do something you cannot. Oooh,” she savored the thought, “please let it be the last one.”

“You’ll be pleased, then, I think. Condor Moray does have access to military forces, but our *security* is private. And with all the excitement around Violet this year”—Illapa noted the tiniest of winces from Sadiya—“we thought it best to, let’s call it, diversify. We’re looking to secure manufacture and transport of some of our more sensitive chemical components.”

A gamble, a small one, that Vidya had not been overly precise about the nature of the support needed. No better way to learn about Violet than to buy the same thing.

Sadiya scrutinized her for a moment.

“How marvelous. I was hoping you would answer as such,” she said, standing. “Come, let me show you what you’d be purchasing.”

---

What had seemed like a single office was, in fact, one of perhaps a dozen entryways into the ten-story block of the tower that comprised the Blade’s Nairobi branch. Sadiya, narrating, walked Illapa past the training grounds and the firing range.

“For us it is simply a profession. We are paid by objectives met.”

“Seems you’ve got it cracked. Put the coin in the purse for the right reasons at the right intervals, and you get what you want.”

“Ah! Excellent. This is not so. It is an important part of the equation. But this is what I wanted to show you!”

Their path became louder, not quieter. A guard stepped aside and opened a door. A deafening cacophony of thundering music, fanatic crowds, and animalistic sounds met them, along with strobing lights and the sight of a small sea of bodies below. Box seats, Illapa realized, above a small arena, with dense tiered seating around a central raised dohyo. In the ring, two bloodied, exhausted mercenaries circled one another. Between them was a pistol.

“Is that a gun?”

“It is,” said Sadiya, savoring the response. “A small dart with bullet ant venom. Not fatal. Shoot your opponent and it’s a victory, but a much lower prize. Disable her and get cuffs on her, and you have a full victory. Spectators get shot now and then and are paid for it—but if it happens twice, you probably did it on purpose for the payment, so you get half the amount every time it happens. No one has been shot twice since.”

One mercenary vised her legs around the other’s neck. The other went limp, and the crowd roared.

“Clever training,” said Illapa. “But games are just that.”

“I could not agree more. Our skills are not what I am interested in showing you. Ah, here it is.”

The announcer walked into the ring, and the music quieted, as did the crowd.

“Fellow fighters! In our final fight for the evening, we have two honored guests from city-under! Tonight’s fight is Mismatch!”

Sadiya clapped her hands together in delight.

“First, weighing in at over two hundred kilograms, Ngrumo!”

A colossal silverback gorilla knuckled his way up onto the dohyo. He waved to the crowd, flexed, and beat his chest. The thundering sound sent the crowd into apoplexy.

“Second, weighing in at a svelte twelve kilograms, Kali!”

A lithe white-yellow serval trotted, almost pranced, onto the dohyo. She bent one knee, bowing to the crowd, then reared on her hind legs and bounced, and finally opened her mouth and bared her needle-sharp fangs to the entire room. As she did, a single gleaming drop emerged from one of the canines and dropped to the mat, sending the crowd into a second set of fits.

“Is that serval venomous?”

“She certainly is,” said Sadiya. “An exquisite creature.”

“Strength versus speed and venom?”

“Strength and *smarts*. Both are hybridized, but as you know, if the species is already intelligent the increase is substantial. No uplift, but Ngrumo has beaten some of our dumber recruits at chess.”

A bell toned, and the fighters squared up. The crowd dropped to a whisper.

“Victory,” said the announcer, “is knockout or ring-out. Understood?” She looked to the gorilla, who nodded, then to the serval, who did the same.

“Fighters ready!” The announcer backed off the dohyo. “Fight!”

For several seconds, there was little movement. The gorilla, Ngrumo, crouched slightly, spring-loading himself. Kali, the serval, hunkered in a stalking position, movements achingly slow. Ngrumo feigned a lunge, a hint of a muscle flinch, and Kali was in the air. Pure coiled energy sent her arcing through space, her entire body rotating up and around her head, tail helicoptering, such that as Ngrumo’s hand swept out to grab her, he found emptiness. At the last moment Ngrumo shifted his weight, dropping his arm and shoulder just out of range, sliding across the ring. The serval landed, and the two were once again still, having replaced one another on opposite sides of the ring. The tense crowd was silent.

Sadiya stood, as if she could not tolerate the excitement, but then said, “Come on, that’s enough of that.”

Bewildered, Illapa followed her back into the corridor.

“Was the fight over?” Illapa asked.

“No, not at all. What did you think of it?”

“I think I want to know how it ended!” she said, somewhat indignant.

“Excellent. *That* is what I had hoped to show you,” Sadiya said, stepping into an elevator. Illapa felt gravity increase as they ascended. “What you encountered in Koryo was a single team. But we are not uniform. What you are hiring, in effect, is not any given team, but our expertise in matching you to the right team for the job. Which, in your case, is protecting shipments. Product.”

---

The elevator opened at one end of a long corridor walled in glass. Beyond it, in a huge room like a warehouse, several detached train cars sat in various states of unloading.

“Is this part of the city-mid station?”

“It is. We have access to our shipments the moment they arrive. For this particular client, whose needs are so close to your own, we had a ‘pick-up team’ who retrieved the shipment from the original source, rode with it by sea and rail, and are now switching teams. The nature of the product is… sensitive, complex. And this may not even be its final destination, so we needed a team comfortable moving through, shall we say, extra-national conditions.”

“We could do the same,” Illapa said, feigning thinking aloud. “Ship from Inka to the Mombasa port, bring it up here, shift teams to rail specialists.”

“Of course. Everyone specializes in something.”

“Like ogumo?”

Sadiya stiffened, the way one does when stepping into icy water.

“I’m sorry,” she said, offering the agent an out. “I believe I misheard you.”

“You didn’t,” said Illapa. “Ogumo. Or something very much like it. Along with the moto, one of my ‘discoveries’ at the Summer Palace was a chunk of armor. We didn’t quite believe it ourselves.”

“Ogumo no longer are made. War-relics, once, yes, but none exist now.”

“So we thought!” said Illapa. “But the cells were too fresh. The suit would have been made in the last decade, no more.”

“Where are you going with this?”

Illapa looked out at the train cars, considering. “The sovereign corporations are something new. We, the Inka, need Condor to win. I want to hire you precisely because of your experience working with them.”

Again, Sadiya winced.

“With whom?”

“With Violet, of course. I was not *entirely* honest in my reason for interviewing you,” said Illapa. “We believe Ward Hyeon and her Sifu have gone missing. My intel says the Ward was in Pyongyang the night following the attack and so, claim eyewitnesses, was the Sifu, but not the Princess.”

“Viceroy-elect Prince Ji-woo already has a bounty on all three.”

“And, given the nature of this situation, I imagine the Blade has been privately offered a second, far more lucrative bounty on only the Ward and the Sifu. Let us offer a third.”

Sadiya scrutinized Illapa, then said, “Leave Inka. Come work for us.”

“Work for the Blade, as a mercenary?” Illapa could not keep the disdain out of her voice.

“Your partnership with the Keralans and the Olokun’s spy network”—it was Illapa’s turn to wince. Sadiya grinned slightly and continued—“is hardly much different. You’re on loan! Nations, mighty as they are, can’t last. They are organized around accidents of chance. There is nothing magic about the dirt in which you were born, nothing tragic about that of others. Demanding your service, wrapping it in the trappings of honor and duty, rather than earning it, is unworthy of you.”

“And for that I should defect?”

“Such strong words, Agent! No no no, not defect. The Blade is not at war with Inka. We are not a nation. Violet, the Blade, the Onzulu—these are competition for Condor, not mortal enemies of the Inka.”

“They sure do a lot of killing for corporations.”

Sadiya shrugged. “These are violent times. Look at Prince Ji-woo! Allied with Violet to achieve a near-bloodless coup for both of them. And you!” She laughed, surprising herself with the idea. “A critic of violence. How often our anger at others is anger at their reflection of ourselves. Alas, I cannot accept your offer on behalf of Condor, Agent Quechua. Our prior contracts stipulate as much.”

“And I will not leave Condor, nor the Inka.”

“You may still,” Sadiya said, an ironic smile forming, “but sadly I agree it will not be today.” She depressed a button on the wall. A moment later, a black cat padded into the hallway, sat, curled its tail around its feet, and regarded Illapa with yellow eyes.

“Iyawa here will show you out.”

“That easy, eh?” Illapa asked, eyebrow raised.

Sadiya laughed. “I am no fool, Agent. You know *far* more than I would like, but you arrived that way. Killing you would put me in a difficult position with *several* major powers, and I do have a *company* to run. Best of luck in your hunt. You will need it. My people have a substantial head start.”

She offered a small bow, which Illapa returned. Then Sadiya entered the elevator and was gone.

Illapa took one last look at the station, tilting her watch toward the glass and depressing the crown twice, then turning slightly and pressing it again.

Below, the crates were unloaded under more guns than the agar cars beside them. A client whose needs were so close to her own. Chemical. The lemur’s word had been *chem*. The Kingdoms and Zhongguo, talking to make a thing and then talking to move it, off the books. A mercenary house riding it by sea and rail for a client whose name made Sadiya wince. No one guarded medicine like this. Whatever was in those crates was not meant to heal anyone, and it was moving.

She did not have a word for it.

She turned to the cat. “After you.”
