At thirteen, Msizi had been ordained as an apostle-in-training for the Cult of the Outer World. The name had started out a pejorative, mocking anyone deluded enough to want to leave the walls of the Onzulu while the war outside still raged. A century on, the Cult held one of the Onzulu’s six executive seats, and only cult members could leave the arcology. Which worked out well, because they were often the only ones who ever *wanted* to.

And Msizi had wanted to, so very badly—not only the sky and the savannah but the *peoples*, millions agreeing with one another and disagreeing with every other million. She studied the Outer Arts for a decade and assisted a veteran rep on thirty journeys. Her first solo was a gentle one: attend the Koryo enthronement. Show up, be nice, pay attention, enjoy the festivities. What could go wrong?

She cursed her mentor for that hexing question as she shivered against the metal door of the walk-in fridge. Two full days and nights had passed. They—she, Illapa, Eshma—had been abandoned, or perhaps sacrificed, to the thing on the other side of the door.

Eshma, to Msizi’s amazement, had been the first to name it. *Preta*, she called it, a hungry ghost. When it was noted that it was more corporeal than most ghosts, she huffed and began to raise her voice. Here, Illapa’s ability to encourage quiet by being more terrifying than what was beyond the door proved quite effective. Eshma relented—*ghūl*, then, she offered, a graveyard feaster. An angry jinn, warped and twisted, consumed by a need to eat. Msizi had begun to roll her eyes when Illapa’s serious nod gave her pause. There had been rumors of such things from the war, and even a few still used in the Revolutions after.

Illapa’s final lucid guidance was to watch it. Don’t poke your head up, she said, drawing her hand over her nose and mouth as if peering over a fence. Lean from the side. Your forehead is a bigger target than your cheek. Watch that thing. Take notes.

Then the fever hit. The thing had polluted Illapa’s hand as much as it had damaged it. In the back bend of the fridge they built as much of a nest as they could. Eshma was a poor partner in a crisis—she seemed forever on the verge of tears—but she understood how to make anything comfortable. For a full hour, while mopping sweat from Illapa’s brow and half smothering her when the nightmares came, she studied the shelves. Then condiment containers were emptied into the tub between the shrink-wrapped veat steaks. What, Msizi had begun to ask, would we need large, empty, sealable buckets for in here—ah, yes. Biology is unrelenting, even in crisis.

And so Msizi and Eshma watched, jotting notes on the back of the inventory clipboard by the door, sleeping in two-hour snatches.

Their first observation, before Illapa lost consciousness, was that something else had called the ghul away. There were crashes and shouts. Then the bodies came, dragged one by one into the kitchen—five, Msizi guessed, but she was too horrified to truly count. It ran on pure instinct, like an ant cutting a leaf. Whatever humanity had been, this vile automaton was now bereft of it. Most teeth were missing. The skin had hardened like the calluses on a heel. Rent cheeks exposed the few remaining teeth back to the molars, an ear-to-ear rictus in an abandoned graveyard of a smile. But it was not dead.

With methodical intention, it searched the kitchen, its movements a sleepwalker’s uncanny mix of dexterity and confusion. A large cleaver and roasting trays were set on the center prep counter. Two huge stockpots, each nearly the size of its torso, were filled with water and set to boil. A corpse was dragged onto the counter. Savage, clumsy blows struck the shoulder until the arm detached. Butchery ensued. Each body was dismembered, the pieces sorted into piles of arms, hands, legs, feet, torsos, and heads. Thighs went onto the roasting trays and into the hot oven. Heads, hands, and feet went into the stockpots, now at rolling boils, billowing steam.

Exhausted by its efforts, the thing lay flat on its back, lidless eyes rolled back in their sockets. Msizi had expected the breathing to slow; instead, it accelerated. The pale blue-gray skin became ruddy. Wounds began to seep. Ten minutes, perhaps fifteen, and it sat up.

Now the thing turned to its demonic cookery. Bare-handed, it removed the trays from the oven—the searing was audible. Msizi did not look. She only listened: the clinking, warping sound of cooling metal; the fizz-popping of water trapped beneath; and the sound of eating. Ravenous tearing, greasy lip-smacking, and great gulping swallows filled her ears like screams.

When the sounds slowed, she dared the window. It was pulling the last strings of meat from a femur. Thus denuded, the bone went into one of the great steaming pots. The belly was distended, the tray empty. It slumped to the floor and slid again into its peculiar brand of panicked sleep. Msizi caught herself looking at Illapa, whose breaths were just as rapid, her sleep just as fitful. No. She would not consider that. Not yet.

Nearly an hour passed before it stood, lethargic and stiff. The ballooned gut was now a mere paunch. Worse, it was no longer skeletal: the cheeks were full, the hips and ribs buried. It wandered to a corner and evacuated. Hot, steaming black ichor spattered the floor and walls. Msizi fought a retch.

For two days now, the cycle had continued, the cadence of its ghastly circadian three hours, as long as five. With every waking it appeared stronger and, somehow, more horrific. Calluses became spurs and plates of bone. Corpsish musculature grew, like a leper engaged in bodybuilding. New, jagged teeth jutted from its jaw. Now, at random, it would approach the fridge door and scrape at the handle. When a talon did hook, the can of beans would creak in inanimate protest.

Illapa’s fever had stabilized. Msizi no longer feared she would die. The damp cold had suffused into their bones. As the ghul’s strength grew, their safety waned. There was no waiting out this thing. Escape would be their only option.

Wake up soon, Msizi said, wiping the fevered brow of the Inkan. We need you.

---

On the fourth day Illapa awoke out of a fever dream. Cold sweat clung to her face. Her hand still burned, but now only flares of pain shivered through her. As her mind crawled out of the grogginess, it stumbled across a handful of facts: she was still alive, which had been in question; she was damp and cold, so they were still in the refrigerator; the can of beans Msizi had wedged into the door handle remained; Moon—what he had become—was still outside. The earthy tang of early decay permeated the air.

Illapa went to speak, but a hand pressed over her mouth—Msizi’s.

“The ghul is still outside,” Msizi whispered, her lips brushing Illapa’s ear. “We have to be quiet.”

Illapa nodded. Msizi removed her hand.

“How long has it been?”

“Four miserable days and nights,” said Eshma, adjusting herself within her cocoon of blankets. “I have eaten more herring than I ever wish to again.”

Msizi held out a plastic bottle of blue liquid. “It’s an exercise drink. Not water, but between this and the coconut milk we’re surviving.”

Illapa drank. The cracks in her lips stung and the paste in her mouth thinned. Four days, by the Sapa. She should be dead. A scratch on the hand from that thing had nearly killed her.

“What do we know?”

“It cooks them,” said Msizi. “It’s growing. Bones coming through.”

Illapa’s eyes narrowed as she burrowed into her memories. There had been stories of cannibalistic monstrosities from the war, but nothing like this.

“This is a weapon of some kind,” she said. “A war-relic level threat.”

“No one is coming,” said Msizi.

“Then I need to get back to my room.” Eshma and Msizi turned to her, disbelieving. “Which means opening the fridge and dealing with that thing.”

“Even if,” said Eshma in a hissing whisper, “we *could* get the fridge door open quietly, even if we *could* sneak past the horrific culinary experiments while the monster slept. Why? For what?”

“I have no gun. The creature is, by now, superhuman in size and strength.”

“But it seems,” said Msizi, “like it’s suffered intense brain damage?”

“*Right.* It is also dumb, and getting dumber. So we have to outsmart it. A thing with basic drives is easy to trick.”

“It can cook!” said Eshma. “It can plan!” Both Illapa and Msizi put fingers to their lips. She rolled her eyes, mouthed a sarcastic silent apology, and continued, “It must have been designed to survive alone.”

“But it was not likely designed to survive long,” Illapa corrected. “Things like this, as best I know, were used to infect an *enemy* soldier. You still want it to die at the end, just to take as many of its compatriots with it as possible in the process. As it gets stronger, it gets more bestial. We rely on that.”

“How much stronger can it get?” Eshma asked.

“There is still a lot of…” Illapa hesitated. “Meat out there. I suspect we are at the beginning, not the end. If we don’t get out, it will, eventually, get in.”

For a moment all three were quiet as their imaginations painted separate, gruesome scenarios with a similar palette.

Eshma, chastened, said, “Well, that’s not how I planned to go. So let’s get going.”

Half-naked, huddled in sheets and wood shavings, surrounded by dewy produce, hanging meats, and shelves of cans and jars, they began to scheme.

The sounds in the kitchen had stopped for, as best Illapa could guess, twenty minutes. Silent as a ghost, she crept to the door and peered through the window. No ghul. She pressed her face to the glass and nearly stumbled backward. It had put itself to sleep almost directly in front of the fridge door. They would have barely two feet of play to squeeze out. It must have suspected.

She signaled as much to Msizi, but they were go all the same. With agonizing care, she removed the can from the handle, the whispers of scraping metal like screams. The rapid rise and fall of the creature’s chest told her it was still in its metabolic catalepsy. Illapa would open the door. Eshma would hold it in that precise position as Illapa left, then drift it all but closed until she returned.

With a gentle push, Illapa depressed the lever and leaned against the door, putting every effort into controlling her movements. The sound of the rubber seal peeling away from the metal may as well have been the ripping of a ship in half. Go slow, be careful, watch. Her face never left the frame of the door as it swung open millimeter by millimeter. For all three, opening the door felt as though it took somewhere between three minutes and three millennia. The heat from the room poured through the gap, and with it the umami-sweet pork smell of cooked people. Involuntary saliva flooded their mouths.

Illapa signaled, and Eshma, trembling, took the lever and did her best impression of a statue. Illapa turned sideways and slipped through the gap, a hand’s breadth from the heaving chest of the ghul, and was gone into the dark.

---

Regret, that emotion of the spineless, had somehow snuck its way into Illapa’s brain. Action requires mistakes, and better to have acted and wished one had not than the other way. Yes, she thought, do not regret acting.

With much fervor and gusto she thought this thought.

Its efficacy was remarkably diminished, however, by the crashing and denting of the refrigerator door. The can of beans having proven insufficient, she had enlisted Msizi and Eshma in reinforcing the damaged door with the shelving all around them. When the creature smashed its bone- and tooth-encrusted fist into the door, it buckled less, though it rattled a good deal more. And when it did break through, it would need several additional seconds to claw through the shelving.

I might be reaching, she considered, too hard for a silver lining here.

“Are we going to die?” Msizi asked.

“No, I suspect not,” said Illapa, bracing another girder against the door. “It stopped cooking. It’s just gulping broth now. We rattled it.”

Another wall-shaking smash and the door buckled further. Screeching, rending metal was followed by a subtler sound, one that continued after the steel settled: gasping, almost gargling. With it came that warm, tantalizing scent that drew saliva unwillingly from their ducts. Where the door had buckled in, where the indentation met the frame, a gap the size of a fist had opened. Eyes, one bloodshot and wild, the others deformed and wriggling at the tips of disjointed fingers, stared through it. Then came a far worse sound. Laughter, like a hacksaw through a carcass, each ragged outward breath dragging against the ruined vocal cords and deformed tubing that kept the thing alive.

A second set of teeth, misgrown and arbitrarily arranged, had breached its gums, doubling its horrific rictus. It stared at her, she at it, as if each were attempting to read the other’s mind. Then it backed up and returned to the pot, where it once again quaffed great mouthfuls of broth. The rest, scalding hot, splashed on its arms and chest. Blisters formed instantly, swollen purple-white.

“Serpent feathers, it doesn’t feel pain,” said Illapa. “It’s probably breaking its bones with every charge. I’m not sure how many of those hits we can take.”

Eshma was in full panic, insisting she was too young to die, and Illapa was a spy, wasn’t she? What was that gun good for if not shooting the thing? Shoot it! Shoot! Monologuing her own anxiety seemed to neither assuage nor activate her fellow captives.

“Sorry, darling,” said Illapa in a *what are you gonna do?* tone. “This”—she held up her pistol—“is meant for killing humans. That out there isn’t any more. I’d just punch a few holes in it and make it angry.”

It charged again. The door shrieked, the gap tore wider, and a miserable, mutated arm thrust through. Msizi, close enough to feel it sweep through the air, brought her own makeshift weapon down upon it. It was part of a shelf, not much better than a spear, one end bent into a sharp hook. It hit and embedded. Neither the arm nor the creature reacted, save for a pause, as though startled. It drew the arm back out, Msizi’s weapon still embedded. Blood, black and clotted, flowed out of it in clumps like spoilt milk. Intermixed was the yellow-green-pale ooze of a wound where the immune system is fighting a losing battle. The smell was overwhelming, like roadkill after four hours in the blazing sun, both septic and caustic.

“We’re going to die here, aren’t we?” Msizi whispered.

Illapa looked at her, then back to the door. She offered no disagreement.

“You’re not wrong, but don’t go saying it out loud. And don’t go asking me as if I have a better answer.”
