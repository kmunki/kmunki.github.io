The sea did not stop. That was the first thing Sun had learned about the room, and the only thing she had not found a use for.

Everything else she had inventoried. A low bed, its linen cool and fine and changed each morning while she was elsewhere. A chair grown from a single piece of woven bio-plastic, which had replaced the wooden one she broke on the first day against the window. The window had not broken. It ran floor to ceiling and looked east over a crescent of white sand and a sea so clear it was less water than a lens laid over the reef. The walls were a green someone had chosen to be calming. The door had a handle on her side and no lock. The lock was on the other side.

A good room. A room built for a guest by people who knew exactly how a guest differed from a prisoner, and had put the whole difference in the door.

She sat on the edge of the bed, facing the glass, hands loose in her lap. It had taken two days to find the posture. Rage was a servant, and a poor one in close quarters; she had sent it to wait outside. What remained was attention.

Footsteps in the corridor. Two sets—the guard’s, heavy at the heel, and another she did not hear so much as fail to hear.

The air changed before the door opened.

Warm, faintly resinous. Cedar, and something beneath the cedar that her nose could not name and her shoulders apparently could, because they loosened. A pleasant weight settled behind her sternum, the feeling of a bath drawn by someone who loved you. Her body was agreeing to something her mind had not been asked. That was not trust. That was chemistry, and chemistry was a tool, and someone was using it on her.

The door opened. Not the guard. The asura, carrying the tray herself.

Hideyo. Sun had learned the name on the second day and the rest of her on the first: cropped black hair, the white right arm, eyes ink-black to the lids with no whites at all. She set the tray on the desk at Sun’s elbow—rice, a clear broth, half a papaya, a roll still breathing steam, a small knife laid across the rim of the plate—and turned to go without a word.

Sun did not look at the tray. She looked at the sea. Her eyes went to the knife once, a flick, the way one checks the clock in a long meeting.

At the door, Hideyo stopped. She did not turn.

“That knife’s balance isn’t good for throwing. You’re welcome to try, of course, but then I’ll have to take it, and you’ll have to tear your roll like a savage.”

For a moment the only sound was the sea.

Sun threw it anyway. Low and flat from a seated wrist, the way the Sifu had drilled into both of them, and it was a good throw. The knife was light in the handle, as promised, and came round a half-turn early. Hideyo tipped her head perhaps a finger’s width. The knife struck the doorframe pommel-first and clattered on the tile.

Hideyo sighed. She picked it up, walked back to the desk, and laid it across the plate exactly as it had been. Then she looked at Sun—properly, for the first time—a beat longer than the moment required. The way a smith looks at an edge that has not taken the temper.

“She’s safe,” Hideyo said, to the corridor.

Li Xing rolled in first. Sun had seen the face on a hundred screens; in person it was smaller, and more awake. The chair had lowered itself to seated height, so that they met eye to eye. Behind her came Miriam Okafor in a dress the orange of a persimmon, a white lab coat folded over her arm as though she had been called from the bench and meant to return to it.

“Dr. Wen,” said Sun. “Dr. Okafor.” Her voice came out level.

Both women bowed—Miriam from the waist, Li Xing from the shoulders. A real bow, not a nod.

“Princess Yi Seong-ma of the New Joseon,” said Li Xing. “Thank you for receiving us.”

As though she could have refused. As though there were a household to announce them. Sun inclined her head the precise degree a royal without a throne owed a foreign head of state, and watched Li Xing see her do it.

“You are angry. Confused,” said Li Xing. “You should be. But not at us.”

“Not at you.”

“We could lie to you,” said Miriam.

“I advised them to,” said Hideyo, from the door.

“We thought it better to state reality,” said Li Xing, “and see how you handle it.”

“Reality.” Sun let the word sit. “Your creatures came into my home in a storm. My guards died on the stairs. A thing with no face carried me out into the rain. And now the two most admired women alive bow in my room and tell me where to aim my anger. Is that the routine? The asura frightens me a little, so that I am relieved to see you?”

“Partly,” said Hideyo.

Li Xing did not look at her. “We did not come for you.”

Sun felt the arm again, the one that had lifted her off the moto—a pressure around the ribs, gone as soon as she noticed it. She kept her hands in her lap.

“Then whom?”

“Your sister. Hyeon. We had the records, and the records could not tell the two of you apart. We took the Progeny. It was the reasonable guess.”

“You thought I was her.”

“We thought you were her. We were wrong. Hyeon is what the Society bred for. You were the mistake.”

The Society. Sun filed the word without touching it.

“Then I’m the wrong parcel,” she said. “Return me.”

Miriam laughed—once, surprised out of her—and covered it.

“A mistake is not the same as worthless,” said Li Xing. “Agar was a mistake. Half of what grows on this island began as one. Your Viceroy told you your chances of Selection were too small to merit calculation.”

Sun went very still.

“You had someone in the room.”

“We had someone in every room but your rearer’s.” Li Xing rolled a little closer. “She was wrong, and she was also right. Your chances were small because the thing you wanted was small. What is a Viceroy? A woman a hundred cousins agree to put in a chair so the dirt they were born on stays theirs. Koryo is an accident of dirt, Seong-ma. Here, every citizen chose to come, and was chosen back. A nation with a purpose instead of a border.”

“And I was chosen by a thing with no face.”

“We did not steal you first.” Li Xing let that land. “The Joseon picked you from a tray of embryos, and at five they handed you to a foreigner with instructions. You have been someone’s purpose your entire life. I am offering you your own.”

It would have been easier if the woman were a fool. Sun felt the offer go in warm, and named the warmth for what it was: the sound of being taken seriously, which she had wanted so long she could no longer hear it without leaning toward it.

“Walk with Miriam tomorrow,” said Li Xing, turning her chair. “The beach is better in the morning.”

When they had gone, Sun buttered her roll with the knife. It was a very good roll.

She had hated the first walk. The second morning a guard came for her, only a guard, and the corridor smelled of nothing but floor polish. The asura had gone somewhere. Sun could think of one place with work for a woman like that just now, and filed it.

Miriam walked barefoot at the tide line, sandals swinging from two fingers, lab coat over the other arm. The path from the beach climbed past a schoolroom open on three sides to the sea wind. Twenty-odd children sat cross-legged while a teacher drew a cell on a board, its organelles in colored chalk. One small boy had fallen asleep against the post, and no one was waking him. Farther on, on the veranda of a clinic, a clinician knelt beside an old woman’s chair with two fingers on her wrist, and both of them were laughing at something Sun could not hear.

She had seen these pictures in the broadcast from Uplift and assumed they were staged. Nobody stages a sleeping child.

“Ji-woo is Viceroy-elect,” said Miriam, as if resuming a conversation. “The council gave him twelve days before enthronement. How is he being received? What will they tolerate?”

Sun kept walking. “Is it not dangerous to tell a prisoner this?”

“You’re not a prisoner. You’ve been reared for someone else’s purposes. We’re…” Miriam frowned at the sand, hunting the word, and found it with a small embarrassed laugh. “Unrearing you. That’s a terrible word. Li Xing does the words.”

It was not performed. Sun had watched a great many people perform candor, and candor performed was always a half-beat early. Miriam’s was late, and clumsy, and real. Sun noticed it. Then she noticed herself noticing, and liked that less.

“Where is Hye?”

“Safe as anyone is, I think.” Miriam looked out past the reef. “Out of Koryo, last we heard. The tide’s turning. We should go back before the rocks.”

She had answered a smaller question than the one asked. *I think. Last we heard.* Which meant they had heard, and recently, and it was not finished. Sun read her the way she had read Ji-woo in the long hall—he announced his secrets by the volume at which he hid them. Miriam hid hers by looking at the horizon. Sun filed that beside the empty corridor.

“Who spoke for him?” she asked. “At the emergency session.”

“A cousin. Duri.”

“Duri.” Sun almost smiled. “Duri cannot finish a toast. Every councilor who heard that speech knows someone wrote it. Three things. He is never to be seen with Duri again. Not at dinner, not in a corridor. Duri is the seam, and whenever they stand together the council sees the stitching.”

The sandals had stopped swinging.

“Second. Jaehwa and Myung-suk are still being questioned in Busan?”

“Yes.”

“Let him bring them home. Himself, publicly. Then, in full council, he offers to put the vote again with them present.”

“He might lose.”

“He won’t. They’ll decline. They have to, in that room, with his guards in the hall. And their refusal is worth more than any vote. No one in Koryo trusts a Viceroy who wanted it. Selection is legitimate when it looks reluctant. He was begged—that’s the story. Let them watch him offer it back.”

“And third?”

“Keep Daewoo. He promised her an honorable retirement to her face. If he makes good on it now, it’s a purge, and every councilor over sixty will count themselves next. Put the Vizier on the dais beside him. Let her be seen to be his.”

Miriam looked at her for a long moment.

“That’s very good,” she said.

“I know.”

The pleasure of it was physical: a heat along the jaw, the clean give of a key turning in a well-made lock. From her side, for once. In Koryo, no one had asked her anything but whether she was ready yet. She hated how good it felt, and hated it precisely, and did not stop.

Walking back, she counted what she had given. Ji-woo, a coronation that would look like humility. And, in the same breath, the two best candidates in Koryo returned to the capital, and Daewoo at his elbow, close enough to hear everything he said.

Beside her, Miriam had begun to hum.

In the evening the sun went down behind the mountain, and the sea turned from glass to slate. Sun had looked too long at the glare that afternoon; green and violet spots still drifted on the dark water wherever she set her eyes, blind places the shape of the thing that had made them.

*What the Society bred for.*

Of course it was Hye. Hye, who had offered to haggle with an arcology for the chance to see one. Hye, whose knee Sun had gripped under the Viceroy’s gaze the day the Viceroy sent them to the edge-lands. Hye had never asked which it was, excitement or terror. Sun had known. It had been both.

They had taken the wrong sister. They would go for the right one. The asura was already gone.

Fear came to the door. Sun told it to wait.

She sat with her hands in her lap until the last light left the water, and the spots faded, and the sea was only the sea again, turning over in the dark. Sun would rule something—Koryo, or this, or whatever these women were fool enough to put in her hands—and she would use every inch of it to reach Hye before they did.
